## What This Is

The Creative Strategy Engine is the shared strategic layer that sits above execution skills. It organizes creative thinking around pain or desire, audience context, messaging angles, and stage coverage so that planning, generation, briefing, and analysis all speak the same language.

This file is for shared strategy knowledge, not execution craft.

- Generative and brief execution live in the installed core creative skills described by the active package instructions.
- Hook craft and hook-stage expression live in `/agent/brain/creative-strategy-guidance/hook-generation--standards.md`

## When to Use It

Use this framework when the user asks to:

**Planning**

- map or build creative strategy from scratch
- identify strategic gaps in current creative coverage
- organize campaigns by tension, audience, angle, or stage
- understand which messaging angles are worth testing
- plan fuller stage coverage for a brand

**Ad generation**

- establish what the ad needs to be before generating it: which tension it addresses, which audience or persona it speaks to, which stage it is for, and which messaging angle it expresses
- identify which matrix cell is most worth filling when no brief or concept exists yet
- verify that an existing concept or brief maps to a clear strategic position before execution begins

**Analysis**

- explain why certain creative is winning or losing beyond surface metrics
- understand which audience, stage, or angle a message is really working for
- diagnose structural problems such as bottom-heavy coverage, missing early-stage creative, or overreliance on one truth
- identify what to build next based on what is working and where the gaps are

## Choose the Primary Anchor

Every strategy organizes around one of two anchors.

**Pain-first** is the default. Use it when the product solves a clear problem someone already feels, searches for, or wants relief from.

Examples:

- medical or health: cystic acne, poor sleep, chronic fatigue
- specific problem-solvers: bulky wallet, weak WiFi, leaky water bottle
- any product with a clear before-and-after state

**Desire-first** is the exception. Use it when the product is primarily about aspiration, identity, status, taste, or enhancement rather than relief from an acute problem.

Examples:

- luxury or fashion: quiet luxury aesthetic, timeless elegance
- status or identity: collector credibility, early adopter
- enhancement: barista-level coffee at home, hotel luxury in your bathroom

Rule of thumb:

- if someone would search for the problem, start pain-first
- if they would search for the aspiration, start desire-first

This anchor is the organizing principle for the strategy map. It does not prevent an individual ad from using the opposite emotional direction.

Pain-first brands can still run desire-led creative. Desire-first brands can still run pain-led creative. This step decides how the matrix is organized, not what every individual ad is allowed to say.

## Map Audiences and Personas

Do not start with demographics. Start with tension.

An audience is a group of people who share the same problem, desire, or frustrating situation strongly enough that the same core message would move them for the same reason.

A persona makes that audience legible. It describes the life context, skepticism, and lived experience that change how the message should sound.

The audience defines the shared struggle. The persona helps you picture the human experiencing it. Both are creative tools. The job is to give a creative team enough to write, cast, and direct from without follow-up questions.

### The many-to-many relationship

- one pain or desire can show up across multiple personas
- one persona can experience multiple pains or desires
- each intersection is a distinct messaging-angle opportunity

For each tension, identify 3 to 5 plausible audiences or personas and define:

- the situation that puts them in market
- what they have already tried or tolerated
- what they believe or doubt
- what would make this product feel meaningfully different
- what makes their situation distinct from other people experiencing the same anchor

If you cannot answer those questions, the audience definition is too generic.

The matrix should take shape as intersections, not lists:

```text
                Persona A    Persona B    Persona C
Pain/Desire 1      ✓            ✓
Pain/Desire 2      ✓                         ✓
Pain/Desire 3                   ✓            ✓
```

Each `✓` is a different messaging-angle opportunity.

### Start from tension, not demographics

A tension is the specific friction point that creates purchase intent for this product. It is not "busy parents." It is "parents who have stopped cooking dinner because they are out of ideas by 6pm and too tired to think." The more specifically you can name what someone is experiencing, the more useful the audience definition.

### Existing vs. growth audiences

This distinction matters because it changes the job of the creative.

- **Existing audiences** already believe the category works. They need a reason to choose this brand. Creative for existing audiences can lead with proof, benefits, and differentiation.
- **Growth audiences** do not yet clearly see themselves as customers. Creative has to start in their world before it earns the right to make a product claim.

When mapping multiple audiences, flag which are existing and which are growth. That distinction changes what the creative has to accomplish.

### Are these actually different segments?

When comparing segments, make the difference meaningful. If two segments would respond to the same creative for the same reason, they are the same audience.

Make the contrast explicit. If one is an existing buyer and another is a growth audience, call that out because the creative implications are completely different.

### Audience quality bar

Every audience definition should:

- name a specific situation, not a demographic group
- state the tension in the person's own terms, not the brand's terms
- be specific enough to guide casting, tone, and message selection
- include enough skepticism context that downstream creative work knows what to avoid

A definition that could apply to most brands in the category with minor word swaps is too generic.

## Define Messaging Angles

A messaging angle is the core truth for a specific anchor × audience intersection. It should sound like a real thought a real person could have, not marketing copy.

Examples:

- Bulky wallet × minimalist professional -> "Your wallet sucks"
- Cystic acne × professional burned by dermatologist -> "Your dermatologist wrecked your skin"
- Poor sleep × exhausted professional -> "Melatonin stopped working three months ago"

To define a strong angle, answer:

1. **Use case:** how does this person experience the anchor in their actual life?
2. **Deepest desire:** what do they really want on the other side of it?
3. **Feature or benefit priorities:** what product truth matters most to them?
4. **Top objections:** what would they doubt first?

Then turn that into one sharp statement that names the tension or belief shift clearly enough to guide execution.

Good angle statements feel like:

- a problem recognized out loud
- a belief challenged
- a cost surfaced
- a payoff made emotionally concrete

Weak angle statements sound like generic benefits or category copy.

Bad: "Professional-grade natural healing without prescription side effects"
Good: "Your dermatologist wrecked your skin"

The good version sounds like something a real person would think. It names a specific insight. It is memorable.

### Messaging-angle template

```text
PAIN OR DESIRE: [Anchor]
AUDIENCE OR PERSONA: [Name and short description]

MESSAGING ANGLE: "[Core angle statement]"

Context:
- Use Case: [How they experience this]
- Deepest Desire: [What they really want]
- Feature/Benefit Priorities: [What matters most to them]
- Objections: [What they doubt]
```

## Frame for Context

Creative framing is how the same strategic move changes when the context changes.

Good framing should change what gets made, not just restate who the audience is.

### For an audience

Adapt to:

- what they have tried
- what has made them skeptical
- what they trust
- what they are tired of hearing

### For a season or moment

Adapt to the emotional state of the moment, not just the calendar label.

Ask:

- what are people preoccupied with right now
- what are they hoping for
- what are they tired of
- what makes the usual category message feel stale in this window

### For a stage

Adapt to what the person already knows and what the creative still needs to move.

- early stages earn attention and recognition before they sell
- middle stages position the solution and differentiate it
- late stages remove doubt and make action feel obvious

If the framing would not materially change casting, tone, proof, pacing, or message emphasis, it is too generic.

### Framing quality bar

- Is it specific to this brand, audience, and context, or could it apply anywhere with minor word swaps?
- Would it actually change what gets made? If a creative team would produce the same work without it, it did not add anything.
- Does every sentence point toward a creative decision? Cut anything that does not.

## Map Across Stages

Use stage to define the persuasive job of the creative, not just to label the funnel.

### Stage 1: Unaware

The viewer does not yet recognize the problem or category clearly.

- strategic job: reveal or reframe the problem
- what usually works: pattern interruption, curiosity, problem discovery, education
- what usually fails: product features, offers, heavy proof too early

### Stage 2: Problem Aware

The viewer knows the problem but is not yet comparing solutions.

- strategic job: validate and intensify the problem
- what usually works: relatable problem language, specific callouts, real frustration
- what usually fails: jumping straight to the product before the pain is fully felt

### Stage 3: Solution Aware

The viewer knows solutions exist and is comparing approaches.

- strategic job: position this solution as credible and distinct
- what usually works: differentiated mechanism, authority, comparison, clear "why this approach"
- what usually fails: generic benefit claims that any competitor could make

### Stage 4: Product Aware

The viewer knows the brand or product exists but still has objections.

- strategic job: reduce doubt and build confidence
- what usually works: proof, reviews, demonstrations, specific objection handling
- what usually fails: broad awareness messaging that repeats what they already know

### Stage 5: Most Aware

The viewer is close to buying.

- strategic job: remove the final barrier and trigger action
- what usually works: offers, urgency, guarantees, direct calls to act
- what usually fails: unnecessary education or long persuasion

Hook-specific stage expression belongs in `/agent/brain/creative-strategy-guidance/hook-generation--standards.md`.

## Read the Matrix

The matrix is:

- anchor
- audience or persona
- messaging angle
- stage

Use it in two directions.

**Planning**

Each empty cell is a possible creative bet. The matrix shows what has not been built yet.

**Analysis**

Map what exists back onto the matrix. That reveals:

- which audiences or stages are winning
- which cells are overrepresented
- which cells are missing
- whether the account is bottom-heavy, top-heavy, or structurally unbalanced

Ask:

- where are the strongest results concentrated
- which audiences are underexplored
- which stages are missing
- whether multiple winners are all expressing the same truth in slightly different clothing

### Pain and audience coverage

- Are there tensions with only one or two audiences explored? That is usually a growth opportunity.
- Are there audiences that appear in only one tension despite clearly having multiple tensions? That is usually an untested intersection.

### Stage coverage

- Is the account bottom-heavy? Then it may be harvesting demand instead of growing it.
- Are Stages 1 and 2 missing entirely? Then scaling will usually be fragile.
- Is there Stage 1 and Stage 4 coverage but little in between? Then there is likely a progression gap.

### Messaging-angle diversity

- How many genuinely distinct angles exist per tension?
- Are multiple executions all expressing the same truth in slightly different clothing?
- Are winners concentrated in one angle type because that truth is dominant, or because the others have not been tested properly yet?

## Budget and Stage Prioritization

Not every brand should invest equally across all stages.

- lower budgets usually extract more efficient demand from problem-aware and solution-aware work first
- scaling budgets need stronger unaware coverage to create new demand
- accounts that only win in product-aware or most-aware territory may be harvesting demand instead of growing it

Treat these as prioritization cues, not rigid rules.

The key to scaling is diverse early-stage coverage across multiple anchor × audience intersections. Broad top-of-funnel coverage is what creates room to scale.

## Handoff to Execution

Once the shared strategy is clear:

- use the installed core creative generation skill to build hooks, concepts, messaging, audiences, and formats
- use the installed core briefing skill to package an approved direction into production-ready execution
- use `/agent/brain/creative-strategy-guidance/hook-generation--standards.md` when the job is specifically hook craft

This engine defines:

- what truth the creative should express
- who it is really for
- what context changes the framing
- what stage-specific job the creative has to do

Strategy without execution is incomplete. Execution without strategy is directionless.
