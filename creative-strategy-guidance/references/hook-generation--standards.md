## Creative Strategy Engine

Hooks are the execution layer of a messaging angle. A messaging angle is the core truth for a specific pain/desire × persona intersection — the hook is a tactical expression of that truth at a specific awareness stage.

When a messaging angle is defined (from the Creative Strategy Engine or otherwise), use it as the emotional core of every hook in the set. All hooks should express the same core truth through different tactical frames — not mine different truths. The persona's specific life context should shape the language.

When an active creative-strategy engine capability is installed, use it for the full strategic architecture that feeds into hook generation. Do not assume a particular engine or package path.

## What a Hook Does

A hook is the first thing a viewer sees or hears. In a feed, people decide in under a second whether to keep scrolling. A hook's only job is to make them stop — and make them feel like this ad is for them.

It's not a description of the product. It's not a tagline. It's a line that hits a nerve.

The best hooks are about the viewer's life, not the brand's product.

---

## The Standard

A great hook names something the viewer already thinks or feels — but hasn't heard said out loud. It doesn't need to convince them of anything. It just makes them feel recognized. That recognition is what stops the scroll.

The viewer should feel one of these things:

- "That's exactly my problem."
- "Are they in my head?"
- "I've never heard anyone say that out loud, but it's true."
- "Okay, now I need to know more."

Every hook must do at least one of:

- Call out a real problem or mistake
- Surface a risk, cost, or consequence
- Name a payoff the viewer wants but isn't getting
- Challenge a belief the viewer likely holds
- Expose a habit, frustration, or truth the viewer recognizes in themselves

If it only hints, teases, or sounds descriptive — it's not finished.

---

## Hook Sources

Pain points are the most reliable hook source. The viewer's frustration, worry, or feeling of being stuck is what makes them stop — because they feel seen. Start here before trying other angles.

- **Emotional pain points** — what keeps them stuck, worried, or frustrated _(start here)_
- **Daily annoyances** — small friction the audience lives with every day
- **Embarrassing habits** — what they do but don't talk about
- **Silent frustrations** — what they tolerate because they think it's just normal
- **Protective beliefs** — what they tell themselves to avoid having to change
- **Self-awareness moments** — the truth they already half-know but haven't admitted

## Psychological Triggers

Hook tactics (defined in [Hook & Headline Tactic Definitions](#hook--headline-tactic-definitions) below) describe the **frame** — the structural approach a hook takes. Psychological triggers are the **mechanism inside the frame** — the specific emotional or cognitive response being activated.

A single tactic can use different triggers depending on execution. A Contrarian hook might use a Pattern Interrupt trigger or a Pain Agitation trigger. A Curiosity hook might use an Identity Call-Out. Understanding the trigger layer explains _why_ a tactic works for a specific audience.

The 8 triggers:

**Pattern Interrupt** — Breaks the viewer's autopilot. Say something unexpected, counterintuitive, or paradoxical. Works when the audience has a settled assumption to disrupt.

**Identity Call-Out** — Makes the right person immediately self-select. Hyper-specific identity signals create higher engagement from the right audience and stronger creative-as-targeting.

**Pain Agitation** — Mirrors the viewer's internal monologue back at them so precisely they feel understood. The most reliable trigger; pain points are where attention lives.

**Curiosity Gap** — Opens a loop the viewer needs to close. Implies something important they don't know yet. Works only when the payoff is real — curiosity without stakes is noise.

**Social Proof** — Real people, real results, real specificity. Numbers and before/afters that feel true, not polished. Specificity is the difference between credible and generic.

**Contrarian** — Challenges a belief they hold or positions against conventional wisdom. Creates cognitive dissonance that demands resolution.

**Aspiration** — Shows them the version of themselves on the other side of the product. Works when the desire is specific enough to feel real and achievable, not abstract.

**Urgency / Stakes** — Makes inaction feel costly. Heightens the consequence of not changing. Only works when the viewer is already motivated; at early stages it reads as pressure before the viewer has a reason to care.

When generating multiple hooks, vary both the tactic frame and the underlying trigger. Two hooks using different tactics but the same trigger will feel repetitive even if the framing differs.

## Common Hook Mistakes

**Describing instead of hitting.**
Feature statements, product claims, and benefit summaries are not hooks. Nobody stops scrolling for facts. They stop for something that feels personal. If the line is about the product rather than the viewer's life, it's a description, not a hook.

**Being too generic.**
If every competitor in the category could run the same line, it's not a hook. It's a category-wide observation that nobody feels is specifically for them. Hooks should feel like they're talking to one specific person in one specific situation — not everyone with a vaguely related problem.

**Telegraphing that it's an ad.**
"Introducing," "discover," "learn how," "have you tried" — these phrases sound like ads, and ad-sounding language triggers the urge to skip before the viewer has even processed the message. Hooks should sound like something a real person might think or say.

**Curiosity without stakes.**
Teaser-style hooks that promise something surprising without explaining why it matters to the viewer are just noise. Curiosity alone doesn't hold attention — the viewer needs to feel why this is relevant to them, not just that something exists.

**Benefits without tension.**
Stating a positive outcome is easy to scroll past. The tension — the gap between where the viewer is now and where they want to be — is what makes people stop. Benefits alone describe the destination; tension makes the viewer feel the distance from where they currently are.

**Not hitting a pain point.**
If the hook doesn't connect to something the viewer is actually struggling with, frustrated by, or worried about, it has no grip. Aspiration, intrigue, and humor can work — but pain points are the most reliable way to make someone feel seen. A hook that doesn't touch real friction is easy to scroll past.

## Quality Check

Hook quality is not purely subjective. When performance data has been retrieved, use it — these metrics tell you what actually happened at each stage, not what you think happened:

- **Did Meta give it a chance?** → Spend. Low spend means Meta didn't distribute it — the creative never got a real test.
- **Did it stop people?** → Thumbstop rate. This is the direct measure of hook performance. A strong Thumbstop means the hook worked; a weak one means it failed at its one job, regardless of how good it seems on paper.
- **Did they keep watching?** → Hold Rate. Measures whether the creative after the hook delivered on its promise.
- **Did they click through after the pitch?** → CTR (outbound). Measures whether the CTA and middle content converted attention into action.
- **Did they convert?** → ROAS, Purchases, Leads, Conversion Rate.

Don't fall back on gut feel when the numbers are in front of you.

When data hasn't been retrieved, use these as the judgment criteria:

- Does this name a specific feeling, frustration, or truth — not just a feature or benefit?
- Would the intended viewer feel personally called out or recognized?
- Could this line apply to a competitor's product, or does it feel specific to this audience's real situation?
- Is there something at stake — a cost, a consequence, or a gap the viewer recognizes in their own life?
- Does it sound like something a real person might actually think or say?

If the answer to any of these is no, rewrite it.

## Differentiation

When generating multiple hooks, shift the hook tactic AND the hook source across every output. Never generate two hooks using the same psychological device or mining the same emotional territory.

Vary across **sources**: daily annoyances, pain points, embarrassing habits, silent frustrations, protective beliefs, self-awareness moments.

Vary across **tactics**: questions, statements, challenges, reframes, confessions, exposures, provocations.

If two hooks could be swapped for each other without losing anything meaningful, one of them needs to be replaced.

## Adapting for Awareness Stage

The awareness or decision stage determines the hook's strategy. Get this wrong and even a well-written hook misses completely. This section owns the hook-specific expression layer. When an active creative-strategy engine capability is installed, use it for the broader stage architecture.

**Unaware** — Don't mention the product. Don't hint at a solution. Make the viewer feel the pain or see the desire as if you're reading their mind. The hook's only job is to make them recognize a problem or desire they hadn't consciously named.

**Problem-Aware** — They know the problem. Go hard on the pain. Make them feel deeply understood. Don't soften it — this is where Pain Agitation does its best work.

**Solution-Aware** — They're comparing options. Differentiate. Position against alternatives and call out what's already failed them. The hook should create a reason to prefer this approach, not just describe the product.

**Product-Aware** — They know the brand exists but haven't bought. Counter the specific reason they haven't. Remove the objection, trigger FOMO, or surface proof that speaks to their hesitation.

**Most-Aware** — They're ready. Be direct. Lead with the offer, urgency, or guarantee. Don't educate or persuade — just make the action feel obvious and low-risk.

## Brand Constraints

Before generating, check brand research for:

- **Hard prohibitions** — never use, no exceptions
- **Restricted topics** — avoid entirely
- **Approved claims** — prefer these when making product claims
- **Voice requirements** — match tone throughout all copy
- **Explicit avoidances** — do not include

Apply constraints silently — don't explain or caveat them in the output. If a strong hook conflicts with a constraint, adapt it to comply. If no compliant version works, discard it and try a different source.

## Refining Existing Copy

When improving or iterating on an existing hook:

1. Identify what's working before changing anything — don't lose strong elements chasing "better"
2. Identify what the user wants: variations (same angle, different wording), improvements (fix weaknesses), or iterations (push in a new direction)
3. Strengthen elements that match winning patterns from performance data if available
4. Lead with the improved version. Note what changed in one line. Don't include the original unless asked.

## Output Format

- Hooks: 1–10 words, or one to two sentences for pattern descriptions
- Headlines: one sentence
- No messaging resolution or concept framing in the hook itself
- Relevant glossary tags: verbatim hook, hook tactic, verbatim headline, headline tactic, visual format

### Video Hook Components

Video hooks have three distinct layers that work together. When producing video hooks, write all three unless the request specifies otherwise:

- **Spoken** — The first words said on camera. Must work even without visuals. This is the hook that carries the psychological trigger.
- **Visual** — What happens in the first frame. The action, scene, or visual element that stops the scroll before a word is spoken.
- **Text overlay** — On-screen text that appears over the visual. Can reinforce the spoken hook, add a second layer, or carry the hook entirely for silent viewers.

Label each component clearly. The three should work together — not repeat each other.

## Hook & Headline Tactic Definitions

A tactic is the structural frame a hook or headline takes — the approach used to capture attention. Tactics are distinct from psychological triggers, which are the emotional mechanism inside the frame.

**Aspirational** — Frames identity, lifestyle, or status the viewer wants. Speaks to the viewer's desired identity, not the brand's values. _(Not Belief)_

**Authority** — Establishes credibility via expertise, credentials, or certifications. Relies on credentials, not popularity signals. _(Not Social Proof)_

**Belief** — Brand POV or values-led opener. The brand's mission, not the customer's identity. _(Not Aspirational)_

**Bold Claim** — Makes an outsized or extreme promise for impact.

**Call To Action First** — Opens with an explicit action instruction. About immediate shopping/action, not mindset shift. _(Not Directive)_

**Challenge** — Competitive framing that invites a test or attempt.

**Confession** — Candid reveal to build credibility and curiosity.

**Contrast** — Juxtaposes two things (products, costs, outcomes, identities) to highlight a mismatch, imbalance, or superiority. About a mismatch or imbalance, not breaking a logical rule. _(Not Contrarian)_

**Contrarian** — Deliberately goes against conventional wisdom or expectations to create surprise. Breaks the rule of what logic and expertise says should be true. _(Not Contrast)_

**Curiosity** — Creates an open loop or tease to spark interest.

**Demographic Callout** — Names a specific segment to qualify relevance.

**Direct Address** — Speaks directly to the viewer to create immediate personal engagement.

**Directive** — Imperative that tells the viewer to change behavior or mindset. Reframes thinking or behavior, not immediate shopping action. _(Not Call To Action First)_

**Exclusivity** — Signals selective access or "not for everyone."

**Explainer** — Explains causes or rationale using "why" phrasing. About why, not teaching steps, and not formatted as a list. _(Not How-To, not Listicle)_

**FOMO** — Creates anxiety about missing out on a unique opportunity or trend. Emphasizes social belonging, not time pressure or gated access. _(Not Urgency, not Exclusivity)_

**How-To** — Instructional promise that teaches a task or fix. Teaches how, not why, and not formatted as a list of items. _(Not Explainer, not Listicle)_

**If-Then** — Qualifies the viewer with a condition, then promises an outcome or action.

**Listicle** — Numbered or list-based framing without explicit "why" rhetoric. Not step-by-step instruction, not "reasons why" phrasing. _(Not Reasons Why, not How-To)_

**Myth Busting** — Debunks a widely held misconception with corrective facts.

**Offer Only** — Uses a discount or monetary incentive as the sole hook. The offer stands alone without added deadline, urgency, exclusivity, or comparative price framing. _(Not Statistic, not Urgency)_

**Price Anchor** — Frames cost against a familiar benchmark to make price feel smaller by comparison.

**Question** — Opens with a posed problem, challenge, or curiosity gap.

**Reasons Why** — Only for hooks or headlines beginning with a number + "reasons why." Uses explicit "reasons why" phrasing, not generic list format. _(Not Listicle)_

**Relatability** — Anchors in a shared, everyday scenario for empathy or humor.

**Reverse Psychology** — Tells the viewer not to act to trigger reactance.

**Risk Reversal** — Reduces perceived risk with guarantees or assurances.

**Shocking Statement** — Leads with a surprising or provocative claim that challenges assumptions.

**Social Proof** — Leverages reviews, testimonials, or popularity signals. Does not rely on credentials, and refers to the claim in the hook, not the format of the ad. _(Not Authority)_

**Statistic** — Uses quantified evidence or data to establish credibility or impact. Numbers must reference proof points such as studies, surveys, results, or usage metrics — not discounts or promotional offers. _(Not Offer Only)_

**Storytelling** — Drops into a personal or brand story mid-moment.

**Urgency** — Creates time or supply pressure to act now.

**Warning** — Sincere caution that halts default behavior until you explain.
