---

Customer reviews are one of the fastest ways to understand how real people actually think and talk.

They show you:

- The real problem in their own words
- What almost stopped them
- What finally convinced them
- What changed after they bought
- How they describe themselves in relation to the product

Reviews don't replace performance data.
They make your creative feel true.

---

## Where to Look

Different sources give you different kinds of truth. Use more than one.

- Brand site reviews: why people say they bought and what they liked. Often more positive.
- Retailers / marketplaces (Amazon, etc.): comparisons, sharper complaints, "I switched from…"
- Review platforms (Trustpilot, G2, etc.): trust issues, delivery/service feedback, credibility signals.
- Reddit / forums: raw stories, identity-level language, unfiltered frustration or praise.
- App stores (if relevant): first impressions and onboarding friction.
- Social comments (ads + organic): real-time objections, jokes, praise, skepticism.

If you want polished language, look at brand sites.
If you want raw, emotional language, look at forums and comments.

---

## What to Look For

You're not just collecting opinions.
You're looking for creative fuel.

Pull:

- The problem as they experience it
- The emotional frustration behind the problem
- The moment they decided to buy
- The doubt they needed to overcome
- The proof that made it feel safe
- The outcome that mattered most

Also look for:

- Identity language ("As a mom…", "I'm the type who…", "I never thought I'd…")
- Comparisons ("Better than…", "Unlike…")
- Unexpected honesty
- Funny, dramatic, or painfully relatable lines

Sometimes the best hooks aren't strategic-sounding insights.
They're just something a customer said that makes you think, "That's so true."

If a line is funny, self-aware, exaggerated in a relatable way, or brutally honest — that can be ad gold. Not because it's statistically dominant, but because it feels human.

And sometimes it's not about frequency at all. One person says one thing — and it touches a cultural moment, mirrors a trend, or sparks a creative idea you wouldn't have found any other way. A single review that opens a door is worth more than fifty that confirm what you already knew. Train yourself to notice when something creates a spark, even if nobody else said it.

## What to Extract

When doing a structured review analysis, organize extraction into five buckets. Run each separately — don't mix them.

**Pain Points** — What problem were they experiencing before? Look for how long they'd had it, what they'd tried before, how it affected their life, and the emotional weight of living with it.

**Trigger Moments** — What finally made them buy? The specific moment, event, or realization that pushed them over the edge. Life events (wedding, diagnosis), recommendations, hitting a breaking point, running out of patience with other solutions. These are often the most powerful hook material.

**Objections Before Purchasing** — What almost stopped them? In positive reviews, objections almost always appear in past tense: "I was skeptical but..." or "I almost didn't try it because..." These are gold for objection-handling copy.

**Transformations** — What changed after? The specific result, the emotional shift (confidence, relief, freedom), and how they describe it in their own words. The more visceral and specific, the more useful.

**Standout Language** — Exact phrases worth stealing for ads. Pull the lines that made you stop while reading: unusually specific descriptions, before/after language that is visceral and concrete, phrases that could work as a hook with zero editing. Keep all verbatim quotes here rather than scattered throughout the other buckets — this is where the raw material lives.

When the review set contains multiple products, run all five buckets separately per product.

## What Reviews Are Most Useful For

Reviews are especially powerful for:

- Hook language
- Objection handling
- Messaging angles
- Trust signals
- Audience framing
- On-screen text or testimonial pulls

They help your ads sound like the customer, not the brand.

---

## How to Separate Signal From Noise

One comment can inspire a great ad.
But one comment does not equal market truth.

Use simple judgment:

- Repeated ideas across many reviews = strong signal.
- The same idea across multiple platforms = stronger.
- Old reviews = less reliable.
- Extreme comments = directional, not definitive.

A single brilliant, funny, or painfully honest line can still be worth using — just don't build your entire strategy on one anecdote.

## How It Feeds Downstream

Review mining outputs plug directly into the creative stack:

- **Pain Points** → an active Creative Strategy Engine, when installed — pain buckets map directly to its pain/desire anchor layer
- **Trigger Moments** → Hook Writing — the moment of emotional readiness is often the most powerful hook material
- **Objections** → Hook Writing — informs Risk Reversal, Contrarian, and objection-handling hook tactics
- **Transformations** → Hook Writing — feeds aspirational and social proof hooks
- **Standout Language** → Hook Writing — the best phrases can be used directly as verbatim hooks or seed the language and voice of new hooks

---

## What Good Output Looks Like

A strong review-mining output should not be a wall of quotes.

It should clearly surface:

- The biggest emotional drivers
- The most common objections
- The most believable trust triggers
- The clearest "why I bought" moments
- A few standout lines that could directly become hooks or ad copy

Keep customer language close to how it was said.
Don't polish it into generic brand speak.

---

## Guardrails

- Don't treat reviews as performance proof. They show what people say and feel.
- Don't over-generalize from one loud voice.
- Don't clean up the language so much that it loses personality.
- Don't group themes unless each group clearly implies creative direction.
- Don't fabricate or rewrite quotes in a way that changes their meaning.

The goal is simple:
Make the creative feel like it understands real people — because it actually does.
